Pony or herd?

When to split, and when to stay a monolith

Four parts. First the general idea of a microservice architecture. Then my experience from a real IoT customer project. Then a live demo in Django. Finally an honest look at when you should and shouldn’t do this.

01

What is a microservice architecture?

And why would a Django developer care?

Let’s start with the basics, and with the thing most of us already have: a Django monolith.

Where we start

The pony: a classic Django monolith

my_project · one process · one deploy

accountsapp
catalogapp
ordersapp
paymentsapp
One shared database
  • One codebase, one deploy
  • Joins and transactions just work
  • Everything scales together
  • One bad deploy takes it all down
This is what most Django projects look like, and that’s a good thing. Several apps inside one project, one process, one deployment, one database. You get foreign keys across apps, transactions, the ORM everywhere, and one thing to deploy. The downsides only show up with growth: you can’t scale the orders part without scaling everything, and one bad migration or bug affects the whole system.

Where we’re going

The herd: the same apps, as services

Frontend / API gateway
accountsown deployown DB
catalogown deployown DB
ordersown deployown DB
paymentsown deployown DB
Message broker · events like order.placed
  • Each service owns its data
  • Ships on its own schedule
  • Talks via HTTP or events
  • Fails on its own (ideally)
Same four apps, but now each one is its own service: its own process, its own deployment, its own database. Nobody else touches that database; if orders needs a user’s email, it asks accounts over the network. A gateway or frontend sits in front and routes requests. Services that don’t need an immediate answer talk through a message broker: orders publishes “order placed”, and whoever cares reacts.

The definition

“…an approach to developing a single application as a suite of small services, each running in its own process and communicating with lightweight mechanisms, often an HTTP resource API.”

James Lewis & Martin Fowler, “Microservices” (2014)

The most quoted definition, from James Lewis and Martin Fowler’s 2014 article. The quote continues: “These services are built around business capabilities and independently deployable by fully automated deployment machinery.” Note what it says: it is still a single application. Microservices are a way to build one product, not a pile of unrelated apps.

In practice

Six traits of a microservice

One business capability

Orders, payments, sensor data: one job per service.

Owns its data

No shared database. Others ask through an API.

Deploys independently

Ship orders without redeploying payments.

Lightweight protocols

HTTP/REST, gRPC, or events through a broker.

Picks its own tools

Django here, FastAPI there, C on the device.

Designed for failure

The network will fail. Plan for timeouts, retries and fallbacks.

If you want a checklist, these six traits come up in almost every description. The first two are the hard ones. Splitting by business capability, not by technical layer, and really not sharing the database. Most “microservices” that hurt are a distributed monolith: separate deployments, but they share a database or must be deployed together.

There are two basic ways services talk. Synchronous: orders calls accounts over HTTP and waits. Easy to understand, and it feels like calling a function, but if accounts is down, orders is down too. Asynchronous: orders publishes an event to a broker and moves on; payments and the mailer pick it up when they’re ready. Much more resilient, but now your data is only eventually consistent and a bug can hide in a queue. In IoT you get async almost for free: devices already talk MQTT or similar. Real systems mix both, as we’ll see in the customer project.

The bill

What you gain, what you pay

What you gain

  • Deploy and scale parts independently
  • Failures stay contained, if you design for it
  • Teams own a service end to end
  • The right tool for each job

What you pay

  • Every call can be slow or fail
  • No joins or transactions across services
  • More to deploy, monitor and trace
  • Local dev and testing get harder
Microservices are a trade. On the left are the benefits, and they are real, but most of them only pay off once you have several teams or very uneven load. On the right is what you pay from day one. Every in-process function call is now a network call that can time out. Your ORM can’t join across services and you lose database transactions. You need deployment pipelines, monitoring and tracing for every service. And running the whole thing on your laptop gets harder.

So far

Start with the pony.
Grow the herd when it hurts.

Martin Fowler calls this “MonolithFirst” (2015). Next: what a herd looks like in a real IoT project.

If you remember one thing from this first part: start with a well-structured Django monolith with clean app boundaries. Split out a service when a concrete pain forces you to: a part that needs to scale differently, a team that keeps blocking another, or a different technology. Martin Fowler wrote this up as “MonolithFirst” in 2015. Now let’s look at a project where the herd already existed.

Ingestion has two roads. Sensor telemetry is small and frequent: the device talks MQTT to Azure IoT Hub, IoT Hub routes everything onto Service Bus, Stream Analytics normalises it, and the result lands on Service Bus topics. Each service subscribes to what it needs. That’s the publish/subscribe part from the theory section, in practice. Images are different: big and bursty, so they don’t go through IoT Hub at all. The camera uploads over FTP into blob storage, and the upload itself is the event: Event Grid notices the new blob and puts a message on Service Bus. Two transports, one message bus.

Each step is an Azure Function with a Service Bus topic in between, so each step scales and fails on its own.

Humans in the loop

Operators verify or correct results in the ControlCenter. Their decisions go back into the pipeline as messages.

The image pipeline is my favourite part, because it has no REST API at all. A new image in blob storage is an event. Step one routes it, step two checks which device and location it belongs to and moves it, step three sends it to object-detection models (YOLO) hosted on Azure Machine Learning endpoints, step four auto-verifies results above a confidence threshold and samples the rest for humans, and step five publishes the results. Every step is an Azure Function, and between every step there is a Service Bus topic. If the AI endpoint is slow, messages simply wait in the queue. And humans are part of the loop: operators verify or correct detections in the dashboard, and those corrections flow back as messages too.

Two outputs. The ControlCenter is the dashboard: a Next.js frontend behind an API gateway, talking to a backend-for-frontend that aggregates the core services. Operators see live device status, history, the AI image feed, and they can send commands. Alerts are deliberately a separate track: the alerts service subscribes to the same telemetry topics, decides per alert code whether to open an incident, and sends email. A scheduled job notices devices that went silent. The important design point: alerts don’t depend on the dashboard. If the frontend is down or being deployed, operators still get their emails.

The ControlCenter isn’t only a screen; it’s also the way back to the device. An operator clicks, the request goes through the API gateway to the backend-for-frontend, to the device controller service, which logs the command and sends it as an IoT Hub cloud-to-device message. It’s fire-and-forget: there is no explicit acknowledgement. How do we know it worked? The device reports its new state as ordinary telemetry, through the same ingestion pipeline. The device controller keeps the last-known state, and the dashboard polls it every five seconds. That’s eventual consistency you can watch with your own eyes: you click, and a few seconds later the state changes.

Conclusions

What the message bus buys us

Fault tolerant

Receiver down? Messages wait on the bus. Sender down? Receivers just idle.

Easy fan-out

A new receiver only subscribes to a topic. Nobody else has to change.

Logging for free

A logging service can simply subscribe to every topic.

Parallel by design

Every message stands alone, so add more AI workers. Hundreds of devices already send in parallel.

Bursts and outages?

The queue just grows. Fix: parallel consumers, or separate input and output queues.

The bus itself down?

No local cache, so the data is lost. A deliberate choice on cost and complexity.

So what does all this buy us? The picture says it: the gate to the pony rides is closed, but nobody gets lost. The queue just waits until the gate opens again. Fault tolerance first: if a receiver is down, its messages simply stay on the bus and get picked up later. If a sender is down, the receivers just have nothing to do; nothing else breaks. Fan-out is trivial: a new consumer subscribes to a topic and nobody else has to change. That also means logging comes almost for free: a logging service that subscribes to every topic. And because every message stands on its own, we can process them in parallel: spin up more AI workers if images pile up. In the real system there are hundreds of devices sending at the same time, so it’s parallel by nature. Two honest questions. What if a receiver is down for a long time, or a burst comes in? In our simple setup, the queue just gets longer and longer. You can add parallel consumers or split input and output into separate queues. And what if the bus itself is down? In this project there’s no local cache or buffer, so that data is lost. That was a deliberate decision by the customer: a local cache would be easy to add, but it costs money and adds complexity.

Downsides

What the herd costs us

43

git repositories

2×

as many Azure services

for the one system from the last section

Hard to find your way

Who sends this message, and who reads it? If you don’t know where to look, you’re lost.

Debugging needs a bus

Services push and pull on their own, with no endpoint to call. To trigger anything, you run a message queue.

SPAs need a translator

The frontend speaks REST, the services speak messages. A Backend-for-Frontend sits in between.

No skipping the queue

Nothing happens instantly. Every message waits its turn.

Now the bill. The system I just showed you lives in 43 git repositories, and roughly twice as many Azure services. That is massive complexity for what is, from the outside, one product. Navigating it is a mess if you don’t already know where to look: who publishes this message, who subscribes to it? Debugging is different too. The services don’t have endpoints you can just call; they push and pull messages on their own. So you can’t “trigger” an action: to debug anything locally, you spin up a message queue and feed it messages. A single-page app speaks REST, not Service Bus, so you need a Backend-for-Frontend that translates between the two. And nothing happens instantly: there is no skipping the queue.

Downsides

When order matters, the bus bites

What we expect

Orderplaced
Paymentcharged
Inventorystock reserved
Shippingparcel sent
Customerhappy

What happens with one retry

Orderplaced
Paymentdown, retry later
Inventorystock reserved
Shippingparcel sent
Paymentcard declined

the retry arrives after the parcel has already left

If services rely on message order, they are tightly coupled, and you can’t just add retries. Keep those steps in one service, or make the dependency explicit: shipping waits for both payment.charged and stock.reserved.

The biggest trap: tightly coupled services. Take a classic shop. An order is placed, payment charges the card, inventory reserves the stock, shipping sends the parcel. On a whiteboard that’s four nice independent services. But they rely on the order of messages: shipping assumes the payment already went through, because it always came first. Now the payment service is down for a minute, and its message goes into a retry. Inventory and shipping don’t know that: they just process their messages. The parcel leaves the warehouse. A minute later the retry runs, and the card is declined. Now you have a parcel on the road and no money. With a message bus you can’t just add retries when order matters. Either those steps belong together in one service, one transaction, the good old Django way, or you make the dependency explicit: shipping waits until it has seen both “payment charged” and “stock reserved”. That pattern is called a saga, and it’s more work than it looks.

Live demo: Pony Express

Mail delivery by magical pony

Enough theory. Let’s build a herd. The classic microservice demo is an online shop, but we just saw why a shop needs extra steps. So instead: a mail delivery service, run by magical ponies.

The rules of Pony Express

01

02

03

05

Why not a shop?

Letters don’t depend on each other. No order, no transactions, so retries are safe.

The rules. Letters pile up at the post office and go into the mailbag: our queue. Ponies take one letter at a time and carry it to the village. Not every trip goes well: one in ten ponies gets distracted by a rainbow and loses the letter, some stop for a very long snack. The post office doesn’t care why. If there’s no receipt within eight seconds, it prints the letter again and puts the copy back into the mailbag. So some houses get a letter twice. That’s fine: what matters is that every letter arrives eventually. That’s called at-least-once delivery. And it works here, unlike in the shop, because letters don’t depend on each other.

Here’s the herd. The post office is a Django project with its own database. When you post a letter, it goes onto the bus with the routing key “letter.posted” and lands in the mailbag queue. Ponies, plain Python, as many as you like, take letters from the mailbag, report “picked up”, and deliver over plain HTTP to the village, another Django project with its own database. Their receipts go to a second queue, read by the postmaster: the same Django project as the post office, but a separate process, a management command. It tracks the herd and reprints overdue letters, that’s the orange line. And the logbook subscribes to everything. No service had to change for it to exist.

Live demo

The whole trick in 20 lines

The pony: take, ack, deliver


for method, _, body in channel.consume(MAILBAG):
    letter = decode(body)
    # The letter is ours now.
    channel.basic_ack(method.delivery_tag)
    publish(channel, PICKED_UP, receipt(letter))

    if deliver(pony, letter):
        publish(channel, DELIVERED, receipt(letter))
    publish(channel, PONY_READY, {"pony": pony})

The postmaster: no receipt, no problem


def reprint_overdue(self):
    overdue = Letter.objects.filter(
        status=Letter.Status.PICKED_UP,
        deadline__lt=timezone.now(),
    )
    for letter in overdue:
        letter.attempt += 1
        letter.status = Letter.Status.QUEUED
        letter.save()
        publish(self.channel, POSTED,
                letter.as_message())

At-least-once delivery: acknowledge early, reprint late, accept duplicates.

That’s really the whole trick. On the left, the pony: it takes a letter from the mailbag and acknowledges it right away, so the letter is gone from the queue. Then it tells the post office “picked up”, travels, and if the delivery works, sends “delivered”. On the right, the postmaster, a plain Django management command with the ORM: every second it looks for letters that were picked up but whose deadline has passed, bumps the copy number and puts the letter back into the mailbag. No distributed transaction, no exactly-once magic. Acknowledge early, reprint late, accept duplicates.

OPTIONAL, IF THERE’S TIME. 7: The bus loses its memory. docker compose stop pony, post 10 letters, then docker compose up -d --force-recreate rabbitmq, then docker compose start pony. The post office still lists the 10 letters as “In the mailbag”, but the bus shows 0: they are gone for good. A recreated container gets a new hostname, and RabbitMQ starts with an empty data directory. The post office only reprints letters a pony picked up, so nobody notices. Same as the customer project: if the bus loses its data, the data is lost.

Live fix A, give the bus a memory. In docker-compose.yml, under the rabbitmq service add: hostname: rabbitmq, and volumes: - rabbitmq-data:/var/lib/rabbitmq. At the bottom, under volumes, add: rabbitmq-data:. Then docker compose up -d. Careful: applying this recreates RabbitMQ once, so do it while the mailbag is empty. Then repeat scenario 7: the letters survive.

Live fix B, the post office is the local cache. Its database still knows every letter, so reprint everything that is waiting:

docker compose exec postoffice python manage.py shell -c "import ponybus; from letters.models import Letter; c, ch = ponybus.connect(); [ponybus.publish(ch, ponybus.POSTED, l.as_message()) for l in Letter.objects.filter(status='queued')]"

The lost letters go out again. Any that were still in the mailbag arrive twice, and that’s fine. Point: a local copy of what you sent is the cheap insurance the customer decided against.

04

Pony or herd?

When one buffed-up pony beats a herd of little ones

So, back to the question from the teaser: should you take your Django from pony to herd?

Conclusion

Pony or herd?

One buffed-up pony

a well-structured monolith

  • One team, one codebase, one deploy
  • Steps depend on each other: transactions just work
  • Load grows evenly: a bigger server will do
  • Background jobs? Give the pony a saddlebag: Celery

A herd of little ponies

microservices on a bus

  • Parts scale very differently
  • Events stand alone, order doesn’t matter*
  • Parts must keep working when others fail
  • Teams or tools need to move on their own

Start with a buffed-up pony. Grow a herd only for the parts that need to scale, fail or ship on their own.

* When order does matter, a saga coordinates the steps and undoes failed ones with compensating actions, like a refund.

So when is one buffed-up pony better than a whole herd of little ones? A buffed-up pony is a well-structured Django monolith: clean apps with clear boundaries, on a bigger server with more workers when you need them. Choose it when one team works on it, when steps depend on each other, like the shop with payment, stock and shipping, where you want a database transaction, not a saga. Choose it when load grows evenly, because a bigger server is far cheaper than 43 repositories. And if you need background work, you don’t need a herd for that: Celery gives your pony a saddlebag, a queue without the herd. A herd pays off when parts really are different. In the customer project, hundreds of devices send telemetry while a handful of operators look at a dashboard: those scale completely differently. The events stand alone, so order doesn’t matter and retries are safe. (The asterisk: when order does matter, like in the shop, a saga can coordinate the steps. Each service waits for the events it depends on, and if a step fails, the others undo their part with compensating actions: refund the payment, release the stock. It works, but it’s real extra work.) Alerts must keep working when the dashboard is down. And the AI pipeline needs different tools than a web app. Pony Express is the same: every letter stands alone, so a herd works. So my advice: start with a buffed-up pony. Grow a herd only for the parts that need to scale, fail or ship on their own. Thank you!
